Learn it from the ground up

0

The toolkit you need for everything elseSupply, demand, equilibrium, surplus, triangles

Demand is a list of how much people want to buy at each price. Higher price → they buy less, so the line slopes down. Supply is how much sellers want to sell at each price. Higher price → they sell more, so it slopes up.

Equilibrium is where the two lines cross: the price at which the amount people want to buy equals the amount sellers want to sell.

  1. If both equations are written Q = …, set them equal: Qd = Qs, solve for P, then plug P back in to get Q.
  2. If both are written P = …, set the two right-hand sides equal, solve for Q, then plug Q back in to get P.
ExampleQd = 120 − 2P and Qs = 3P − 30. Set equal: 120 − 2P = 3P − 30 → 150 = 5P → P = 30. Then Q = 120 − 60 = 60.

Surplus means "the extra value people get":

  • Consumer surplus (CS): the area below the demand curve and above the price. Buyers would have paid more than they did.
  • Producer surplus (PS): the area above the supply (or MC) curve and below the price.
  • Deadweight loss (DWL): value that disappears because some worthwhile trades stop happening. On a graph it is almost always a triangle.
The only geometry you needTriangle = ½ × base × height. Rectangle = base × height. Most exam math comes down to these two.

Kinds of taxesPayroll vs income, sales vs excise, property vs estate, progressive vs regressive

Why governments tax: to pay for government itself, to provide goods and services, for law enforcement, to redistribute wealth, and to change behavior. A tax changes prices, so people buy less of the taxed thing.

Two ways to structure a tax. An ad valorem tax is a percentage of price, like Indiana's 7% sales tax or property tax. A per-unit tax is a fixed dollar amount per item, like excise taxes ($1 per pack of cigarettes, cents per gallon of gas).

Fairness. The key number is the effective rate = tax paid ÷ total income.

  • Progressive: the effective rate rises as income rises (income tax, estate tax).
  • Regressive: the effective rate falls as income rises (sales, excise, VAT). Poorer people spend a bigger share of their income.
  • Proportional: everyone pays the same share of income.
Example: brackets10% on the first $20,000 and 30% above that. On $50,000: 0.10 × 20,000 = 2,000, plus 0.30 × 30,000 = 9,000, so the tax is $11,000. The rate goes up as income goes up, so the system is progressive. Only the dollars above $20k get 30%.
Exam trapA 5% sales tax has the same sticker rate for everyone, but it is still regressive. Compare tax to income. $20k household: 5% × 15,000 = $750, or 3.75% of income. $100k household: 5% × 40,000 = $2,000, or 2% of income.

Tax credit vs tax deduction. A credit cuts your tax bill dollar for dollar (EITC, Child Tax Credit, Adoption Credit). A deduction lowers the income that gets taxed (student loan interest, medical expenses). Hint: if the name has "credit" in it, it's a credit.

Who really pays a tax, and how much is wastedTax incidence, tax revenue, deadweight loss, elasticity, Laffer curve

A per-unit tax drives a wedge between what the buyer pays (Pb) and what the seller keeps (Ps): Pb = Ps + t. Fewer units get traded.

  1. Find the no-tax equilibrium (P*, Q*).
  2. Write Pb = Ps + t. Plug Pb into demand and Ps into supply, then set them equal.
  3. Solve for Ps, then Pb = Ps + t, then the new quantity Qt.
  4. Revenue = t × Qt. DWL = ½ × t × (Q* − Qt).
Worked: Vodka, $5 taxNo tax: P = 30, Q = 60. With the tax: 120 − 2Pb = 3(Pb − 5) − 30 → 120 − 2Pb = 3Pb − 45 → Pb = 33, Ps = 28, Qt = 120 − 66 = 54.
Revenue = 5 × 54 = $270. DWL = ½ × 5 × (60 − 54) = $15.

It doesn't matter who legally pays. Taxing the seller or the buyer gives the same Pb, Ps, quantity, revenue, and DWL. Who writes the check to the government (statutory incidence) differs from who actually bears the cost (economic incidence).

The review slide's graphs: tax on suppliers vs tax on consumersDemand starts at P = 10 and supply at P = 2, and they cross at P = 6, Q = 4. That means demand is P = 10 − Q and supply is P = 2 + Q. A tax on suppliers shifts the supply curve up by t. A tax on consumers shifts the demand curve down by t. Both create the same wedge, revenue, and DWL.
The slide gives no tax amount, so try one: with a $2 tax, 10 − Q = 2 + Q + 2 → Q = 3, Pb = 7, Ps = 5. Revenue = 2 × 3 = 6. DWL = ½ × 2 × (4 − 3) = 1. Both curves have the same slope, so buyers and sellers each bear $1.

The burden falls on whoever is less flexible (more inelastic).

  • Inelastic demand (people must buy it, like insulin) → buyers bear most of the tax.
  • Inelastic supply → sellers bear most of the tax.
  • When demand or supply is inelastic, quantity barely drops, so the DWL is small.

Laffer curve: start at a 0% tax and revenue rises as the rate goes up. Past some rate, quantity traded shrinks so much that revenue falls. At a 100% rate nobody trades and revenue is 0.

ExternalitiesSpillover costs and benefits, the Pigouvian tax, the Coase theorem, cap-and-trade

An externality is a cost or benefit that lands on a bystander who isn't part of the deal. The market ignores it, so it gets the quantity wrong.

Negative (harms others)Positive (helps others)
From productionFactory smoke, river pollutionBeekeeper's bees pollinate the neighbor's orchard
From consumptionSecondhand smoke, loud dogVaccines, education
Market resultToo much gets madeToo little gets made

Vocabulary: M = marginal (one more unit), P = private, S = social, E = external, C = cost, B = benefit.

  • MSC = MPC + MEC. Social cost = the seller's own cost plus the harm to others.
  • MSB = MPB + MEB. With a negative consumption externality the MEB is negative, so MSB sits below demand.
  • Market outcome: private benefit = private cost (D meets S).
  • Social optimum: MSB = MSC.
  1. Market: set demand = MPC and solve for Q_market.
  2. Optimum: set MSB = MSC (add the MEC to the cost side, or subtract it from the benefit side) and solve for Q_opt.
  3. Pigouvian tax = the MEC (the harm per unit). It forces the market to "feel" the harm.
  4. With the tax: buyers pay the price at Q_opt read off the demand curve. Sellers keep that price minus the tax. Revenue = tax × Q_opt.
  5. DWL of doing nothing = ½ × MEC × (Q_market − Q_opt).
Worked: T-shirts, $8 harm per shirtMarket: 60 − Q = 20 + Q → Q = 20. Optimum: 60 − Q = 28 + Q → Q = 16. DWL = ½ × 8 × 4 = $16. Tax = $8, revenue = 8 × 16 = $128.

Coase theorem (named after economist Ronald Coase, Nobel Prize 1991). If property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the people involved negotiate to the efficient outcome on their own, without the government, and it's the same outcome no matter who holds the right. The right only decides who pays whom. Example: the dog is worth $500 to Dick and does $800 of harm to Jane. Since 800 > 500, the dog goes either way. If Dick has the right, Jane pays him somewhere between $500 and $800. If Jane has the right, Dick can't afford to pay her enough to keep the dog.

Slide example: steel plant and fishermanA steel plant pollutes a river and hurts a fisherman's business downstream.
If the plant has the legal right to pollute, the fisherman can pay the plant to cut pollution, as long as he's willing to pay at least as much as the plant loses by cutting.
If the fisherman has the right to clean water, the plant has to pay him to allow some pollution. It will only pay up to the point where his cost from the pollution is more than the plant is willing to pay to pollute.
Either way, bargaining reaches a deal that helps both sides, and pollution ends up at the same efficient compromise.

Government tools:

  • Pigouvian tax (or a subsidy for positive externalities) fixes the price of polluting. The amount of pollution adjusts.
  • Cap-and-trade fixes the total quantity of pollution (the cap). Firms trade permits, and trading sets the permit price. Firms don't all cut by the same amount; the firms that can cut cheaply cut more.
  • Both are market-based, and both internalize the externality.
Abatement with a taxA firm cuts emissions as long as cutting is cheaper than paying the tax, so it stops where MCA = tax. For MCA = 300 − 2E and a $50 tax: 300 − 2E = 50 → E = 125 tons.
Exam trapA Pigouvian tax set equal to the harm removes DWL. It doesn't create it. Also, the price buyers pay usually rises by less than the tax, because the burden is split.

Four kinds of goodsRival and excludable, public goods, the tragedy of the commons

Ask two yes/no questions about the good:

  • Rival? If I use it, is there less for you? (A pizza slice: yes. A tornado siren: no.)
  • Excludable? Can someone stop people who don't pay? (Netflix: yes. National defense: no.)
RivalNon-rival
ExcludablePrivate good: clothes, pizzaClub good: password Wi-Fi, fire protection, internet, an uncrowded toll road
Non-excludableCommon resource: ocean fish, the environment, free street parkingPublic good: tornado siren, national defense, an uncrowded free park

Public goods → free-rider problem. People enjoy the good without paying, so markets make too little of it (under-provision). Think of it as a positive externality. Fixes: the government provides it, public-private partnerships, donations.

Common resources → tragedy of the commons. Everyone thinks "one more fish for me is fine" and ignores the cost to others (a negative externality), so the resource is overused until it collapses. "Everyone owns the lake, so no one takes care of it." Examples: overfishing, polluted Lake Erie, climate change, a messy shared lounge, glass on a public beach.

Why it happens, step by step: property rights are shared, so no one is responsible. Free entry means anyone can keep coming. Once use goes past capacity, rivalry kicks in: every fish I catch is one you can't. The resource is used up faster than it can replenish, and it collapses. Compared with the social optimum, everyone ends up worse off (a welfare loss).

Fixes for the commons:

  • Limit access: the government restricts parts of a forest.
  • Property rights, which make the good excludable: a farmer who owns the grazing land won't overload it with cows.
  • Permits and licenses: fishing licenses.
  • Taxes and fees: a carbon tax makes factories pay for their pollution.
  • Cap the number of users: a national park limits daily visitors to protect wildlife.

Game theoryDominant strategy, Nash equilibrium, prisoner's dilemma

Game theory studies strategic decisions among players who depend on each other, by boiling a complicated situation down to a simple setup. A game has four elements: players (who decides), strategies (the choices available), payoffs (profit, utility, and so on), and rules (who moves when: at the same time, or one after another). Everyone is assumed to be rational and selfish. In a payoff pair (a, b), a belongs to the row player and b to the column player.

  1. Row player: for each column, circle the row player's bigger number (the first number).
  2. Column player: for each row, circle the column player's bigger number (the second number).
  3. A box where both numbers are circled is a Nash equilibrium: nobody wants to switch on their own.
  4. If a player picks the same strategy no matter what the other does, that is a dominant strategy.
Prisoner's dilemmaEach player has a dominant strategy to "cheat" (low price, don't reduce pollution, produce over quota), but if both had cooperated, both would be better off. The equilibrium is (Low, Low) even though (High, High) pays more to each.
How your professor defines it: the Nash equilibrium gives a lower total payoff than another outcome where both players do better. Usually both players have a dominant strategy, but that isn't required. Example: in the review-slide game, the equilibrium (4, 5) totals 9, while (Down, Left) = (5, 7) totals 12, so it counts as a prisoner's dilemma.

Coordination game: (3,3) and (1,1) on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere. There are two Nash equilibria and no dominant strategy. Your best move is to copy the other player.

Ways out of the dilemma: change the payoffs (taxes, fines, subsidies), binding agreements with enforcement (treaties such as the Montreal Protocol), and repeated play.

Penalty exampleSubtract 4 from every "Not reduce" payoff. The table becomes Reduce: (6,6) and (1,4); Not reduce: (4,1) and (−1,−1). Now Reduce is dominant for both, so the equilibrium is (Reduce, Reduce) = (6,6).

Definitions to memorize:

  • Profit = TR − TC. TR = P × Q. TC = FC + TVC, where TVC = variable cost per unit × Q.
  • AR = TR/Q (which equals P). MR = ΔTR/ΔQ. MC = ΔTC/ΔQ.
  • ATC = TC/Q. AVC = VC/Q. AFC = FC/Q. So ATC = AVC + AFC.

A competitive firm is too small to affect the price, so selling one more unit always brings in the market price: P = AR = MR. It maximizes profit where P = MC.

  1. Set P = MC and solve for q.
  2. TR = P × q, TC = plug q into the cost function, profit = TR − TC. Or use profit = (P − ATC) × q.
Worked: AR $15, ATC $12, 200 units, FC $600Profit = (15 − 12) × 200 = $600. MC = P = $15. AFC = 600/200 = 3, so AVC = 12 − 3 = $9.

Break-even happens when P = minimum ATC (profit is 0). The firm shuts down when P falls below minimum AVC. In Figure C2, if P drops to $20, q = 10 and ATC = 20, so profit is $0.

MonopolyMR = MC, then read the price off demand; deadweight loss; barriers to entry

A monopolist is the only seller, so to sell one more unit it has to lower the price on every unit. That makes MR fall below the demand curve.

The MR shortcutIf demand is P = a − bQ, then MR = a − 2bQ. Same starting point, twice as steep. Example: P = 150 − 2Q → MR = 150 − 4Q.
  1. Write MR (same intercept, double the slope).
  2. Set MR = MC and solve for Qm.
  3. Plug Qm into demand, not MR, to get Pm.
  4. Competitive outcome for comparison: P = MC on demand gives Qc.
  5. DWL = ½ × (Pm − MC at Qm) × (Qc − Qm).
Worked: P = 100 − 2Q, MC = 20MR = 100 − 4Q = 20 → Qm = 20, Pm = 100 − 40 = $60. Competitive: 100 − 2Q = 20 → Qc = 40. DWL = ½ × (60 − 20) × (40 − 20) = $400.

Compared with competition, a monopoly sells a lower quantity at a higher price, with P > MC, and creates DWL.

Barriers to entry (how monopolies form):

  • Control of a key resource.
  • Government: patents, licenses (a taxi medallion means fewer taxis and higher prices).
  • Economies of scale, which create a natural monopoly: one firm can serve the whole market more cheaply than two (water, electricity).

Price discriminationFirst, second, and third degree

Price discrimination is "the business practice of selling the same good at different prices to different customers." Why would a monopolist do it? To maximize profit by separating customers according to their willingness to pay (WTP).

The perfect version rests on two key assumptions: (1) the monopolist can charge each consumer a different price, and (2) it knows exactly what each consumer is willing to pay. Then all of the consumer surplus becomes the monopolist's revenue.

  • First degree (perfect): each person pays exactly their own WTP (auctions, personalized pricing). All consumer surplus becomes profit. The firm sells the competitive quantity, so there is no DWL.
  • Second degree: offer a menu and let customers sort themselves (airline classes, bulk discounts).
  • Third degree: different prices for groups you can identify (student, senior, happy hour). The more elastic (price-sensitive) group gets the lower price.

Three conditions: (1) some market power, (2) a way to separate customers by WTP, (3) a way to prevent resale. If students could resell cheap tickets to non-students, the price gap would collapse.

Perfect PD profit = the whole triangle between demand and MCP = 100 − 2Q with MC = 20: the firm sells to Q = 40, and profit = ½ × 40 × (100 − 20) = $1,600.
Third degree: run MR = MC separately for each groupGroup 1: P = 50 − Q, MR = 50 − 2Q = 10 → Q = 20, P = 30, profit = 20 × 20 = 400.
Group 2: P = 30 − Q, MR = 30 − 2Q = 10 → Q = 10, P = 20, profit = 10 × 10 = 100.
Total = $500.
Discrete customersWTP of 90, 80, 70, 60, 50 and MC = 40. Perfect PD: 50 + 40 + 30 + 20 + 10 = 150. Best single price: try each one. At $70, 3 tickets sell, so profit = 3 × 30 = 90, the best. The difference is $60.

Oligopoly, cartels, antitrust, integration

In an oligopoly there are a few firms, each one's decision affects the others, so they behave strategically (game theory).

Cartels (like OPEC) agree to limit output and keep prices high. Each member is tempted to cheat secretly, which makes it a prisoner's dilemma. In a one-time game, cooperation breaks down. In a repeated game, tit-for-tat (cooperate first, then copy what the other player did last round) can sustain cooperation, because cheating gets punished later.

Antitrust laws (Sherman Act 1890, Clayton Act 1914) ban:

  • Price-fixing: competitors agreeing on prices.
  • Resale price maintenance: a manufacturer sets a minimum retail price ("stores must sell our sneakers for at least $150").
  • Predatory pricing: cutting prices to drive rivals out of the market.
  • Tying: selling two products only as a bundle.

Policymakers have to judge whether a practice actually hurts competition or has a good business reason.

Market integration can lead to a monopoly in two ways. Picture the supply chain as a ladder: commodity producer → supplier → manufacturer → distributor → wholesaler → retailer.

  • Horizontal (sideways, on the same rung): buying a direct competitor at the same stage of the value chain. Examples: Disney + Fox (2019), Facebook + Instagram (2012), GrubHub + Just Eat (2020), United + Continental (2010).
  • Vertical (up or down the ladder): buying your own suppliers or distributors at different stages of the supply chain. Examples: an oil driller that buys refineries and gas stations; Apple, ExxonMobil, McDonald's, Walmart, Target.

Poverty and welfareAbsolute vs relative, the Official Poverty Measure, income vs substitution effect
  • Absolute poverty: below a fixed cost of basic needs. The U.S. OPM and the World Bank's $3.00 a day are both absolute.
  • Relative poverty: below some share of what others have, such as 50% or 60% of median income.

Both kinds look at the same basic resources of a household: income, consumption, and wealth.

U.S. Official Poverty Measure (OPM) is based on the Federal Poverty Line: "the level of income sufficient to provide a family with a minimally adequate standard of living." It was created in the 1960s, when a poor family spent about ⅓ of its income on food. So it takes the cost of a minimal diet × 3, adjusts for family size, and updates every year with the CPI.

Counted as income (pre-tax cash)NOT counted
Earnings (wages); cash government transfers (Social Security, unemployment, TANF); educational aid; interest, dividends, rentsNon-cash benefits (SNAP, Medicaid, housing aid); tax credits (EITC); private health insurance

OPM limitations: it leaves out big transfers like the EITC, ignores unmarried partners who share resources, uses a 1960s food-based formula, and is the same everywhere (NYC and New Mexico get the same line).

Welfare programs are "government initiatives providing financial and social support to individuals and families with low incomes, the elderly, or those with disabilities." Designing one means four choices: eligibility (who gets it), aid type (what kind of help), generosity (how much), and duration (for how long). Family structure and household makeup decide eligibility and generosity, with special attention to children in poverty. Programs are meant to "graduate" people: pull them out of poverty and move them from welfare into the labor market.

  • Cash transfer: TANF, SSI.
  • In-kind transfer: goods or services instead of cash, such as food, housing, or medical care. Examples: SNAP, housing vouchers, Medicaid and Medicare.

The slide leaves an open question: how should the government choose between cash and in-kind aid? The usual trade-off, which is general background and not from the slide: cash lets people buy what they need most, while in-kind aid guarantees the help goes to food, housing, or health care.

The two effects (the most-tested idea in this unit):

  • Income effect: the change in what you buy because your purchasing power changed, with relative prices held constant. Slide example: coke gets cheaper, so "I am, in effect, richer, and I can buy both more pizza and more coke." A raise in income works the same way as prices falling. For welfare: when you give someone money, they can afford to work less, so labor supply goes down. Any free money does this, like a UBI or lump-sum payment.
  • Substitution effect: the change in what you buy because relative prices changed, with purchasing power held fixed. Slide example: coke gets cheaper, so every pizza I give up gets me more coke, and I switch from pizza to coke. People move away from whatever became relatively more expensive. For welfare, the "price" is the reward for one more hour of work. If working pays more (EITC in its phase-in range, where the credit grows as you earn), labor supply goes up. If the benefit is taken away as you earn (TANF phase-out, benefits only for people who don't work), labor supply goes down.
Exam trapA UBI is unconditional, so it doesn't change the reward per hour, and there is no substitution effect. It lowers labor supply through the income effect.
